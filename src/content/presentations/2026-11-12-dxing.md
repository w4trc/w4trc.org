At the November 12, 2026 meeting of the Kingsport Amateur Radio Club, Seth Conkin, N4XTT, gave an introduction to DXing and shared stories from operating at home, through remote stations, and from the Caribbean.

## What is DXing?

DXing means making contacts with amateur radio stations in other countries or over long distances. Most of it happens on the HF bands, 160 through 10 meters, though 6 meters can open up for DX when conditions are right.

The best-known goal is the ARRL DXCC award, earned by confirming contacts with 100 DXCC entities. There is no time limit, so it can take one year or many.

The hobby also has roots in shortwave listening. Listeners would tune in distant broadcasters such as Radio Havana Cuba and Radio Moscow, send in reception reports, and receive cards in return, much like the QSL cards hams exchange today.

## Seth's path into DXing

Seth was first licensed as a Technician in 2022 at age 16. Field Day 2022 at Warriors Path provided a first taste of HF, and upgrading to General soon followed. Seth's first DX contact came that October, with a German station during the CQ World Wide contest.

Seth earned DXCC in 2023 and now has 243 entities confirmed and 267 claimed. At least three quarters of those contacts were made through remote stations, because HOA restrictions have limited antennas at home.

## What you need to get started

DXing does not require a big station. It can be done with anything from a 5-watt QRP radio and a dipole to a full contest station with beams and a 1,500-watt amplifier. Seth's first station was a Yaesu FTDX10 and an end-fed half-wave antenna in a tree, and it reached as far as Australia.

- **License:** General class is ideal, since most DX is on HF. Technicians can still work DX on 10 meter sideband from 28.3 to 28.5 MHz, and the band gets especially busy during contests.
- **Remote stations:** If HOA rules or antenna restrictions get in the way, remote operating is a strong option.

## Operating remotely

A remote station lets you control a radio in another location over the internet. Seth operates often through a contest station in Massachusetts and through RemoteHamRadio, a paid service with stations around the country.

RemoteHamRadio runs in a web browser. After you pick a station, the console shows a waterfall, radio controls, antenna and amplifier controls where available, and a logger that can upload to Logbook of the World. All you need on your end is a computer and a headset with a microphone.

Membership comes with per-minute airtime charges that vary by station, so check the RemoteHamRadio website for current rates. Its Youth Program, which first got Seth into remote operating, offers free access to licensed operators 21 and younger.

## Seth's DXing tips

- **Be patient.** A DX station often has many callers at once. Wait your turn and don't send your call over and over.
- **Listen first.** Always hear the DX station's callsign before sending yours.
- **Learn to work split.** Many DX stations transmit on one frequency and listen on another, often 5 to 10 kHz higher on voice. Listen for where they say they are listening and stay within that range.
- **Find the listening frequency on CW.** Tune through the pileup for the station the DX is working. That tells you about where the DX is listening.
- **Log right away.** Contacts may fail to confirm on Logbook of the World or Club Log if your logged time is off, so log each contact as you make it and upload soon after.
- **Check the DX spotting sites.** Spots show who is on the air and on what frequency, making them a valuable tool for chasing new entities.

## J68TT: St. Lucia

Seth has operated from St. Lucia twice, first as J6/N4XTT and most recently as J68TT in August 2026. Getting the second license took about eight months.

From the rental house, a 100-watt radio and a vertical antenna worked Europe and some of Asia on FT8, mostly on 15 and 17 meters at night. Seth also made voice contacts from the station of local ham Frans, J69DS, which has Elecraft radios, an amplifier, and a tri-band Yagi on a high ridge pointed toward Europe.

Seth also activated 15 parks for Parks on the Air, including 10 in a single day, operating from a car with local ham Ernest, J69Z. From one beach, 50 watts into a homebrew vertical easily reached Europe over the salt water.

## VP2VRX: An all-youth contest team

In October 2025, Seth joined an all-youth team in the British Virgin Islands for the CQ World Wide SSB contest. The other operators were Michael, W3MLJ, Brayden, KC1KUG, and Jack, W9RFT. The four met through the RemoteHamRadio youth program.

The team entered Multi-Operator, Single-Transmitter Low Power, running 100 watts with no amplifiers:

- **Run station:** Elecraft K3 with a hex beam for 20 through 10 meters
- **Multiplier stations:** Yaesu FT-710 and FlexRadio FLEX-6500
- **Other antennas:** Verticals for 160 through 40 meters, plus a DX Commander Expedition vertical for the multiplier station
- **Logging:** N1MM Logger+

After log checking, VP2VRX finished with 3,631,713 points and 4,228 contacts, taking first place in North America in its category.

## Traveling with radio gear

- **Licensing varies by country.** A US license does not automatically let you operate abroad. Some countries honor it under the CEPT agreement, but privileges depend on your license class and the country, so check the rules before you travel.
- **Start early.** The British Virgin Islands license was quick and inexpensive, arriving within about 12 hours of the request for a $20 fee. St. Lucia took much longer.
- **Ask about equipment rules.** St. Lucia required advance approval to bring radio gear into the country.
- **Carry your radio on.** Seth packs the radio in a hard-sided carry-on, because checked radio gear too often arrives damaged.
